AI Agents and Multi-Agent Systems: CrewAI and AutoGen
AI agents and multi-agent systems represent a critical area of study in computer science. AI agents use Large Language Models (LLMs) to reason about tasks, plan steps, interact with tools, and execute actions toward a specific goal. Their operational capabilities depend heavily on the underlying model, available tools, and overall system design.
A multi-agent system consists of multiple agents cooperating to solve complex tasks. Each agent can be assigned a distinct role and exchange information with peers to achieve a shared objective. This approach divides complex workflows into smaller activities—such as research, analysis, writing, and verification—supporting parallel work and cross-checking depending on the system design.
CrewAI is an open-source framework tailored for building teams of AI agents. Developers can assign specific roles, goals, tools, and tasks to individual agents. CrewAI centers around two core concepts: Crews and Flows. Crews organize collaborative agent groups, while Flows control workflow execution, managing application state, conditional steps, and event-driven automation.  
AutoGen is another prominent framework for developing multi-agent applications. Its AgentChat API supports diverse conversation patterns, including round-robin interactions and model-selected speakers. However, official repository guidelines indicate that AutoGen is in maintenance mode, with new users encouraged to explore options like the Microsoft Agent Framework.  
Despite their advantages, multi-agent systems face inherent challenges. Agents can produce inconsistent results, duplicate work, propagate errors, or encounter coordination bottlenecks. Furthermore, scaling the number of agents often increases processing time and computational overhead. Consequently, multi-agent architectures do not universally outperform single-agent configurations; performance depends heavily on the specific task, model capability, coordination strategy, and evaluation methods.
Evaluating AI agents requires looking beyond final output accuracy. Key evaluation dimensions include task success rates, planning efficacy, tool utilization, robustness, safety, and cost-efficiency. For multi-agent systems, evaluating inter-agent coordination and communication is equally vital.
In summary, AI agents and multi-agent frameworks provide powerful abstractions for orchestrating automated tasks. For computer science students, understanding their underlying architecture, collaboration mechanisms, limitations, and evaluation methodologies is essential for navigating modern software engineering and artificial intelligence.
